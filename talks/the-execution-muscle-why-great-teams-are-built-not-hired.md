# The Execution Muscle: Why Great Teams Are Built, Not Hired

**Speaker:** Charlotte Mestwerdt (Co-founder and Business Psychologist, Bravely)  
QCon SF 2026 · Keynote · Wed Nov 18, 09:00 · Grand Ballroom  
[Session page](https://qconsf.com/keynote/nov2026/execution-muscle-why-great-teams-are-built-not-hired)

> A business psychologist on why projects stall on people more than code, and three daily habits that build trust, shared ownership and feedback.

## Key points

- In her opening poll, almost nobody said their last slipped project failed for purely technical reasons. Most hands went up for human causes like waiting on decisions or someone stuck in silence.
- Her slides cite DORA 2023, where generative cultures had 30% higher organizational performance, and Google's Project Aristotle, where psychological safety was the strongest of five team dynamics.
- The Relationship Bank Account treats every interaction as a deposit or withdrawal of trust. She said trust speeds up execution because every request is a withdrawal.
- Deposits she named: saying you are stuck in standup, asking for review on unfinished work, owning your mistake first in the retro, and giving the reason behind a decision.
- The Ownership Seesaw: when one person takes over, the others lose ownership, energy and buy-in, and the heavy lifter becomes the team's bottleneck.
- Handing over ownership feels like losing control. Tight-loose-tight leadership restores it: agree the why, what done looks like, boundaries, check-ins and a say-it-back, then let go of the how.
- Feedback should be regular, specific and asked for. Her SAFE structure is say what you saw, add why it matters, find out their view, and explore what is next.

## The talk in brief

Charlotte opened by asking who had seen a project slip for purely technical reasons. Almost nobody raised a hand. Nearly everyone raised one for a human cause: waiting on a decision, someone stuck who didn’t say so, two people who didn’t talk. Her argument is that execution is a muscle teams can train, and that it depends less on who is hired than on daily habits. She grounded it in two studies: DORA’s finding that generative cultures have 30% higher organizational performance, and Google’s Project Aristotle, where psychological safety was the strongest of five team dynamics.

She then gave three habits, each with a short exercise for the room. The Relationship Bank Account treats every standup, review and incident as a deposit into trust or a withdrawal from it. The Ownership Seesaw shows what happens when one person carries the load: the heavy lifter becomes the bottleneck and everyone else’s muscle wastes away. Tight-loose-tight expectations and the SAFE feedback structure keep handed-over work on track. Her closing ask: start on Monday with one deposit, one shift on the seesaw and one piece of feedback.

> “If the leader does all the lifting, the muscle wastes away.” (Slide 30)

### The research: how matters more than who (slides 7–9)

Slide 7 puts three findings side by side. From the DORA State of DevOps Report 2023: organizations whose teams have a generative culture (high trust, low blame, information including bad news flows freely) have 30% higher organizational performance. She stressed that generative here has nothing to do with generative AI. From Google’s Project Aristotle: psychological safety is the number one dynamic, and how a team works together matters more than who is on it.

Slide 8 lists what Google expected to matter and crossed it out: individual performance, seniority, tenure, team size, working in the same location, extroversion, consensus decisions and workload. None predicted how effective a team was. What did, in order of importance:

|-----------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------|
| Psychological safety  | Safe to speak up and admit mistakes. Underpins the other four. |
| Dependability         | We do what we say, on time                                     |
| Structure and clarity | Clear roles, plans and goals                                   |
| Meaning               | The work matters to us                                         |
| Impact                | Our work makes a difference                                    |

### Habit 1: the Relationship Bank Account (slides 10–24)

The first exercise: pair with a stranger and find three things in common, with no talking about work (slide 10). That, she said, was a deposit. Every relationship, from a neighbour to a colleague you speak to once, has a bank account. Connecting, keeping a promise and giving useful feedback are deposits. Asking for things, rushed criticism and reacting from stress are withdrawals. In the positive, a curt reply barely registers. A common mistake is to start a new team or onboard someone by going straight to content and expectations: starting in the minus. Then requests take longer, meet more pushback, and people say yes but do no.

Her core claim (slide 12): trust speeds up execution, because execution requires withdrawals. When people trust each other they share problems early, hand over ownership without checking and re-checking, and give honest feedback (slide 13). Low trust brings workarounds, second-guessing and unspoken doubts (slide 14).

She explained why using a three-part brain model she had also presented at QCon two years earlier (slides 15 and 16). An emotional brain quickly decides whether someone is an ally or an enemy from micro-signals. Ally lets the rational brain run, which she linked to high-quality work, innovation, compromise, ownership and connection. Enemy hands control to a survival brain (fight, flight, freeze), linked to defence, blaming, avoidance, procrastination and conflict. In her words, the survival brain always wins, however experienced you are. Deposits steer the emotional brain toward ally.

Then a reflection: what is your balance with the two or three people you depend on most (slide 17)? And concrete examples:

| Moment                  | Withdrawal                              | Deposit                                                                                                         |
|-------------------------|-----------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Standups and daily work | Dismissing an idea in standup           | Say what you’re stuck on, not only what you’re doing. “I’m blocked and unsure” makes it safe for others.        |
| Reviews                 | Criticism before connection             | Ask for review on your own unfinished work. A rough draft signals it’s safe to be imperfect.                    |
| Incidents and mistakes  | Blaming in the incident review          | Own your mistake first in the retro. When the senior person goes first, others learn mistakes are safe to name. |
| Planning and decisions  | Missing a commitment without a heads-up | Give the reason behind a decision, not just the decision.                                                       |

Source: slides 18 to 22.

Slide 23 sums it up: every standup, review and incident is a deposit or a withdrawal, and teams in trust debt pay the interest in delayed approvals, unnecessary meetings and rework. She said owning a mistake makes a person look more professional, not less, and pointed to research without naming it. Trust also leads into the next habit: it is a key ingredient of ownership (slide 24).

### Habit 2: the Ownership Seesaw (slides 25–37)

A run of polls (slides 26 to 29): think of a project from the last three months you felt responsible for. Did you end up fixing something that wasn’t strictly yours? Were you the one checking everyone’s work before it went out? Are you tired just thinking about it? Then the turn (slide 30): your team’s biggest bottleneck might be you. If the leader does all the lifting, everyone else’s muscle wastes away. The second pair exercise asked what each person held on to that someone else could have owned, and what stopped them handing it over.

After a standing-up exercise to make the room feel an imbalance of power (only partly recorded), she presented the seesaw. In balance it means connection, trust, autonomy, regulation, effectiveness and resilience: high performance (slide 32). Balance behaviours include expressing needs, setting boundaries, speaking up about imbalance, putting group goals over ego, repairing after conflict and long-term solutions (slide 33). Out of balance (slide 34):

| Side                  | Goes down                                                                                                          | Goes up                                                    |
|-----------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------|
| The one taking over   |                                                                                                                    | Stress, disconnection, responsibility, control, loneliness |
| The ones pushing back | Control, ownership, autonomy, energy, buy-in                                                                       |                                                            |
| Both sides            | Stress and panic, endless questions, focus on the wrong detail, disagreeing from a fixed mindset. Low performance. |                                                            |

Why is handing over so hard? Slide 35, the “science of control”: control calms the stress response by making things predictable; autonomy is a basic need; and the ownership effect means we value things more when they are ours. Handing over real ownership, not just tasks, increases effort and commitment (slide 36). But to the person giving it, it feels like a loss of control (slide 37). Her fix is to regain control through clear expectations and feedback.

### Habit 3: expectations and feedback (slides 38–45)

Tight-loose-tight leadership (slide 38): clear on the why, free on the how, close the loop. She said it works on any project, however small, and for anyone seeking buy-in, not only managers. The first tight is setting expectations, with five things to agree before you let go (slide 39):

1.  The why
2.  What “done” looks like
3.  The boundaries
4.  The check-ins
5.  Say it back, so the other person states the plan in their own words and takes ownership

Then you consciously shift down the seesaw. The last tight is feedback. Her do’s (slide 40): give it regularly; keep it specific and factual, with no “always” or “never”; ask for it proactively so people don’t go into a stress response; ask “how would you like it served?”; and describe what the person could do more of. The structure is SAFE (slide 41):

| Step                    | Sounds like                                                |
|-------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------|
| **S**ay what you saw    | “I see / hear / notice…” or “It seems like…”               |
| **A**dd why it matters  | “The effect on me / the team / the project is…”            |
| **F**ind out their view | “Do you recognize that?” “How’s that for you?” And listen. |
| **E**xplore what’s next | “My tip would be to…” or “Maybe you could do more of…”     |

The third challenge: think of your workplace nemesis and draft a piece of feedback about something they could do more of (slide 42). She suggested writing feedback out against the four steps before the conversation. A QR code (slide 44) linked to written guides for all three methods.

**Start on Monday.** Make one deposit, with the dependency whose balance is lowest. Shift the seesaw on the project that exhausts you. Give one piece of feedback using SAFE.

## Terms and ideas

Every framework and study the talk names, in plain English.

### Research

[DORA](https://dora.dev/report/2023)  
DevOps Research and Assessment, Google Cloud’s long-running research program on software delivery. Its 2023 report found generative cultures linked to 30% higher organizational performance. Its [2025 report](https://dora.dev/dora-report-2025/) on AI-assisted development found AI amplifies an organization’s existing strengths and weaknesses.

Generative culture  
From sociologist Ron Westrum’s typology, used by DORA: high cooperation, messengers aren’t punished, failure leads to inquiry. Not related to generative AI.

[Project Aristotle](https://rework.withgoogle.com/en/guides/understanding-team-effectiveness)  
Google’s internal study of 180 teams, published on re:Work in 2015. It found how a team works matters more than who is on it, and named five dynamics led by psychological safety.

[Psychological safety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_safety)  
A shared belief that a team is safe for interpersonal risk: speaking up, asking for help, admitting mistakes. The term in team research comes from Amy Edmondson.

### The three habits

Relationship Bank Account  
Her version of Stephen Covey’s [“emotional bank account”](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_7_Habits_of_Highly_Effective_People) from *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*: trust in a relationship rises with deposits and falls with withdrawals.

Ownership Seesaw  
Her model of shared ownership. One person taking over and others pushing back is an imbalance; balance means trust, autonomy and resilience.

Tight-loose-tight leadership  
A management pattern: tight on purpose and expectations, loose on how the work is done, tight on follow-up and accountability.

SAFE feedback  
Her four-step structure: say what you saw, add why it matters, find out their view, explore what’s next.

### Psychology behind them

[Triune brain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triune_brain)  
Paul MacLean’s 1960s model of three layered brains (reptilian, limbic, neocortex). Popular in coaching; rejected by modern comparative neuroscience. She used a version of it to explain why threat shuts down collaboration.

[Autonomy as a basic need](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-determination_theory)  
Central to self-determination theory, which treats autonomy, competence and relatedness as basic psychological needs.

[Ownership effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endowment_effect)  
Also called the endowment effect: people value things more once they own them. Her point was that owned work gets more effort than assigned tasks.
